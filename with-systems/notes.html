How the numbers happened

Taming a scheduler thundering herd

The problem

Spark workloads processing about 350 TB a day were landing late. The p99 landing time was over 9 hours, and runs launched at peak hours were far slower than the same runs launched off-peak.

What I found

I lined up Spark and YARN metrics, queue dashboards and application logs by launch hour. The jobs weren't simply short on compute. They were flooding the scheduler with bursts of executor requests at nearly 4x the queue's capacity. At peak, executor allocation went from minutes to over an hour, and preemptions forced repeated retries, which made the bursts worse.

YARN queue under Spark executor requests · illustration

Before: bursts of executor requests at ~4x queue capacity. Allocation stalls and preemptions churn executors.

waiting 0 · running 0/24 · preempted 0 waitingrunningpreempted

What I changed

  • Halved the Spark thread pools that produced the bursts, so requests arrived at a pace the scheduler could serve.
  • Added vCore headroom for the workload.
  • Retuned queue priority and preemption so running executors stopped getting evicted.

The tradeoff

A small, deliberate increase in compute cost in exchange for predictable landing times.

The result

p99 landing time dropped from 9+ hours to under 5.

The fix was the shape of the requests, not just the amount of capacity.

SLA misses: −78%

From "did the job run?" to "did the customer get their data?"

The problem

Monitoring was run-level and dataset-wide. It could tell us a job had run, but not whether a specific customer's data had landed within their SLA. Some issues were first noticed by customers or account teams rather than by us.

Phase 1: respond faster

As lead DRI for a reliability lockdown, I started with response: SLA-attribution dashboards and tighter on-call and acknowledgment workflows. SLA misses caused by our platform fell 78%.

Phase 2: measure what the customer sees

Next, monitoring moved from infrastructure to customer-level outcomes:

  • Freshness checked against each customer's SLA, not just whether a run happened.
  • Per-customer staleness, so one stale customer can't hide inside a healthy dataset.
  • Detection of silently retrying workflow steps, instead of waiting for long timeouts.
  • Entitlement and billing reconciliation, to catch paying customers without access.
  • Health checks on external warehouses and upstream dependencies.

Paging without the noise

More signals shouldn't mean more pages. Alerts are deduplicated and carry blast-radius context, so one upstream outage produces one actionable page instead of one per affected dataset.

Knowing a pipeline ran isn't enough. The question is whether the customer got their data on time.

Load once, deliver twice

The problem

The platform delivers scheduled datasets into customers' Snowflake accounts. Every destination ran the full physical path on its own: build a manifest, prepare and partition the data in Hadoop, stage files in S3, COPY them into Snowflake, then audit and publish.

One large customer had two destinations with exactly the same contract: the same tables, schema and run date, for the same ten active accounts. So every run did the same preparation and the same load twice.

What I found

Each destination's identity was tied to both the physical work and the customer-facing delivery. Only the delivery has to stay separate: each destination needs its own database objects, permissions, audit and share. The preparation and load don't.

The design

  • An exact-equivalence gate. Two destinations share a load only if everything matches: the same warehouse account and region; the same dataset, schema, cadence and run date; the same tables and delivery configuration; and exactly the same active accounts for every table.
  • A safe default. Any mismatch, and both destinations run independently, exactly as before. If a paused account comes back for one destination, the fallback happens on its own.
  • One canonical load. One destination owns a single manifest, partitioning pass, stage and COPY.
  • Separate delivery. The other destination gets a zero-copy clone, and each runs its own audit and publish.
  • No customer-visible change. Same delivery plans, object names, permissions and shares; no migration and no opt-in.

The tradeoff

Fanning out through shared views instead of clones would also save storage, about $30K more a year. Clones were the proposed route; views remain an alternative.

The result

Estimated savings of about $636K a year (about $53K a month: roughly $43K in warehouse compute and $10K in Hadoop preparation). A hackathon prototype of the shared path used about 29% fewer transfer compute credits than separate loads.

Share the physical work. Keep every customer's delivery independent.
